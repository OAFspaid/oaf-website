<p class="wp-block-paragraph"></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I couldn’t sleep.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Someone I once considered a brother had hurt me — deeply — and it triggered something raw in my nervous system. Disrespectful comments about my service put me in a rage and I saw red. I found myself in the front yard in the middle of the night, trying trying to stay grounded but feeling lost. I wanted to calm down.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I wanted to just do my job.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But I was stuck — and I could feel it. It was like being trapped inside a system that wouldn’t let me out, andI recognized the feeling. It was the same nervous-system firestorm that had led me to the hospital 10 years ago. Not when I was in it… but what came before.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That sense that something was about to break. That my body couldn’t take much more. I was there again. Fully activated. Braced for impact. And in that moment, I knew:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I couldn’t do it anymore. So I quit.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I walked away from the job, the uniform, the “brotherhood” — everything I had built my life around. Because staying was going to destroy me. That morning, when my kids came downstairs and I told them… they smiled. They were happy.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I always thought they loved the fire department — the uniform, the pride, the identity. But maybe they saw what it was doing to me. And in that moment, I knew — I had made the right decision.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A few months earlier, I had been driving Zoey home from speech therapy when a car swerved into oncoming traffic on I-10 — right in front of us. Three vehicles collided. Fire. Screaming. People trapped. I had my daughter with me. I was close enough to help. I had a fire extinguisher in the truck. But it wasn’t enough. Still, I pulled closer. I ran back and forth — checking on Zoey, then checking the scene.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One man — the driver who caused it — was already gone. Fully involved. One car was catching fire. A man inside was conscious, his wife slumped over beside him. I’d later learn she didn’t survive. Civilians were trying to help. One of them had their daughter stand with Zoey while I tried to do something — anything — with no tools, just a voice.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When the fire department arrived, I gave a full size-up. But the damage was done. To the victims and to my daughter. After that, Zoey had nightmares. And Gabe — my son — apparently realized for the first time: Dad could die. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“Yeah bud, fire is hot enough that it will kill you.” I was shocked he hadn&#8217;t realized this, but I guess he just thought fire would just burn you badly. I’m not sure, but I think that&#8217;s when my kids didn&#8217;t want me to do it anymore…</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I still loved the job, but I was unraveling. I was starting Operation Antifragile while helping others in need and was stretching myself too thin. I had been sick for weeks. I couldn’t eat without my body violently reacting.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Everything I ate seemed to go right through me. I didn’t know why.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Later, I’d learn that even the sound of my phone going off — tones, alerts, or vibrations — was enough to send my body into a full-blown stress response. It triggered intense GI distress, muscle tension, panic. My nervous system had been trained over years of trauma:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The firehouse: jolted awake by tones and lights. It was rare I would actually sleep at the station.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Marines: sleep was tactical — never deep, never safe. Often awake doing high adrenaline missions at night.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The hospital: a nurse would shine a red headlamp in my face and force pills down my throat before I would realize what was happening.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That’s what my body remembered. So now, even silence had tension. Even rest felt dangerous. The next morning, I told Lori I was going to meditate and try to sleep. She was taking the kids to school and heading to her parents.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But she was worried about me — for good reason.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I tried to rest, but I couldn’t. My mind was buzzing. My body was buzzing. I couldn’t stop moving. I was actually feeling happy in a way. I was excited about the future and focusing on coaching. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">No more getting up in the middle of the night for random calls that aren&#8217;t really emergencies…</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">No more firehouse drama…</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">No more seeing death and trauma on a regular basis…</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But I was also sad about not being able to help people anymore through firefighting. Eventually, I got up from my bed, got in the shower and started singing Black Flag&#8217;s song Nervous Breakdown out loud:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“I’m about to have a nervous breakdown…My head really hurts. If I don’t find a way out of here…I’m gonna go berserk.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It was coming out of me like a warning siren.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I wasn’t just singing it — I was living it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“I’m crazy and I’m hurt…Head on my shoulders, it’s going… berserk.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When Lori came home and heard me, she panicked. She thought I was losing it and needed to go to the hospital.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I don’t blame her. I probably did need to go somewhere. But, at the time, we thought I could handle it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I was confused. Disoriented. Delusional. I didn’t even know what day it was. For some reason, I thought something had happened to Gabe and Zoey. I was worried they were hurt somewhere. Later, when I tried to lie down, things got worse.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I kept hearing phantom fire tones — in the walls, in my ears, in my bones. Each one sent violent spasms through my body. Lori was right there with me trying to help, but unsure of what to do. I couldn’t rest. I couldn’t sit still. My body refused to shut off.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But then… something happened.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the middle of the chaos, a voice cut through.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Not out loud — not external — but deeper than thought.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A knowing. A calling.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Maybe it was God.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Maybe He had been guiding me the whole time — even when I was screaming, even when I was sick, even when I was trapped in that hospital 10 years ago.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Because what I heard was clear:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“You have a new mission now.” It was Operation Antifragile.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That was the shift.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A breakdown that became a breakthrough.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A purpose born from the ashes. This is how I can continue to help people.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I knew I was done with the fire service.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">And I knew I had to build something — not just for me, but for others who were stuck like I had been. Something for those starting these careers as well.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Veterans. First responders. Tactical Professionals. Trauma Surgeons and ER Techs. Anyone fighting battles no one else could see.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Now, it’s all coming to life.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Operation Antifragile is real. And it’s going to save lives — because it was born from the fire.</p>
