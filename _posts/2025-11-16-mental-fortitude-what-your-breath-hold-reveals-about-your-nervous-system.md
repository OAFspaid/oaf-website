<p class="wp-block-paragraph">By: Matt Spaid</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead of relying on recovery gadgets, I frequently turn to an inherently accurate, built-in system: <strong><em>my breath</em></strong>. Since my teenage years, I have engaged in breathwork and mindfulness training, making me quite attuned to my body&#8217;s signals. This is why it is called a &#8220;practice&#8221; &#8211; consistent effort yields improved results.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most people look at breath-holding as a “skill”, which in some ways it can bem, but in reality, it’s one of the most powerful <strong>nervous system diagnostics</strong> tools. It requires no equipment, no cost, just your own physiology giving you feedback…and it is incredibly accurate.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you practice breathwork, you may have noticed that some days your breath holds feel more difficult—shorter, more uncomfortable, or the urge to breathe comes sooner. This isn&#8217;t a sign of weakness; it&#8217;s simply <strong>data</strong>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Even if you don&#8217;t practice breathwork, you might occasionally notice this effect, though you may not be as conscious of it. When your system is under stress, your breathing often becomes shallower and more rapid. Breathing directly influences your nervous system and Heart Rate Variability (HRV). Specifically, inhalation increases your heart rate (HR), while exhalation decreases it. Consequently, a longer inhale duration will lead to a higher overall heart rate. Irregular breathing patterns will disrupt the balance of both your nervous system and HRV.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your breath-hold tolerance is a direct reflection of:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>CO₂ sensitivity<br></li>



<li>Parasympathetic tone<br></li>



<li>Stress load<br></li>



<li>Sleep quality<br></li>



<li>Recovery status<br></li>



<li>Overall nervous system balance<br></li>
</ul>



<h1 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Why Breath-Holds Drop When You’re Stressed</strong></h1>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There are a few key physiological systems influence breath-hold performance:</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>1. CO₂ Tolerance</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your urge to breathe is triggered primarily by CO₂, not by lack of oxygen.<br>When you’re stressed or underslept, your chemoreceptors become more reactive.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Research shows</strong> that elevated stress increases chemoreceptor sensitivity to CO₂, which reduces breath-hold capacity.</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><em>Noble &amp; Hochman, 2019 – Stress states heighten CO₂ chemosensitivity in the brainstem.</em><em><br></em></li>



<li><em>Fatouleh &amp; Macefield, 2013 – Anxiety increases ventilatory response to CO₂.</em><em><br></em></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stress prematurely triggers the brain&#8217;s need for air. Therefore, training to increase your CO2 tolerance can lead to improved performance and greater resilience to stress.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>2. Autonomic Nervous System State (HRV Connection)</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Breath-holds rely heavily on <strong>parasympathetic dominance</strong>; the calm, restorative side of your nervous system. I find breathwork in the sauna to be incredibly beneficial. By maintaining a calm, controlled demeanor and extending my breath holds, I override my body&#8217;s natural urge to move and breathe faster in the face of discomfort.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When your nervous system is taxed:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Heart rate rises<br></li>



<li>Vagal tone drops<br></li>



<li>HRV decreases<br></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Multiple studies show that <strong>parasympathetic tone and breath-hold duration are positively correlated</strong>:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><em>Miller et al., 2020 – Higher HRV predicts longer breath-hold performance.</em><em><br></em></li>



<li><em>Stanley et al., 2021 – Breath-hold duration is a noninvasive indicator of autonomic regulation.</em><em><br></em></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When your HRV drops, your breath-hold almost always drops with it. Therefore, improving your breath holds can also lead to improved HRV scores. However, this is not due to the breath hold itself. It’s because the <strong><em>training</em></strong> that improves breath holds also improves HRV.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When you train breath-holds correctly, you’re essentially training:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>CO₂ tolerance<br></li>



<li>Parasympathetic activation<br></li>



<li>Vagal tone<br></li>



<li>Diaphragm control<br></li>



<li>Stress regulation<br></li>



<li>Mindfulness under pressure<br></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">All of these are <strong>proven contributors to higher HRV</strong>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Supporting research:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Slow breathing and breath control increase HRV (Lehrer &amp; Gevirtz, 2014).<br></li>



<li>CO₂ tolerance training improves autonomic flexibility and reduces anxiety (Noble &amp; Hochman, 2019).<br></li>



<li>Breath-hold training enhances vagal tone between holds (Bain et al., 2018).<br></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The mechanism is the <strong>breathing practice</strong>, not the max hold itself. So again, consistent training is key.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>3. Mechanical Tension in the Breath Muscles</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your diaphragm, intercostals, and deep core muscles tighten when your nervous system is in sympathetic mode.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This changes how efficiently you can:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Fill the lungs<br></li>



<li>Expand the ribs<br></li>



<li>Relax into the hold<br></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Research shows the diaphragm responds directly to emotional stress and sympathetic activation:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><em>Jerath et al., 2015 – Stress increases diaphragmatic tension and disrupts efficient breathing patterns.</em><em><br></em></li>
</ul>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>4. Cognitive Load &amp; Threat Perception</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your brain interprets breath-holding as a mild form of “threat.” You are purposely creating stress, but it is a controlled stress. If your stress levels are already elevated, your tolerance for further stress is diminished. In this case, incorporating breath-holds into your training regimen might not be advisable. Instead, prioritize restorative breathwork, utilizing a 1:2 inhale-to-exhale ratio (for example, a 4-second inhale followed by an 8-second exhale).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Studies confirm that cognitive load and anxiety shorten breath-hold duration by increasing perceived threat:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><em>Dempsey et al., 2014 – Anxiety reduces voluntary breath-hold time due to heightened threat response.</em><em><br></em></li>



<li><em>Sheel et al., 2016 – Breath-hold endurance drops when the amygdala is activated.</em><em><br></em></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Mindset and nervous system state matter more than lung capacity. It is often the mental side of things that makes you take a breath, not the physical.</p>



<h1 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What This Means for You</strong></h1>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If your breath-holds feel off, it’s not random.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It means your body is communicating:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>“Hey, we’re running hot. Slow down.”</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is one of the cleanest, simplest self-regulation tools you can build into your daily routine. If my breath holds aren’t going well for the session, then I know I need to focus more on my recovery.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Just like you track:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>HRV<br></li>



<li>Morning heart rate<br></li>



<li>Recovery scores<br></li>



<li>Grip strength<br></li>



<li>Sleep quality<br></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You should also track:<br><strong>Breath-hold quality.</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It’s one of the most sensitive indicators of stress and recovery.</p>



<h1 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How to Respond When Your Breath-Hold Drops</strong></h1>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead of forcing harder CO₂ training, shift into recovery-focused work:</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ HRV breathing</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">6-second inhale 6-second exhale<br>5- 10 minutes</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ Nasal-only conditioning</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Keep training lighter and aerobic.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ Longer exhales</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">4-6 second exhales to increase vagal tone.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ Mobility + “Gap” work</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Restore the diaphragm, ribs, and thoracic spine.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ Zone 2 conditioning</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Steady-state work boosts mitochondrial function and recovery.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ Red light therapy + sauna</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Support the parasympathetic shift.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>✔ Focus on sleep</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Breath-hold performance drops immediately after poor sleep.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is how you train <strong>smart</strong>, not just hard…part of the Antifragile method.</p>
